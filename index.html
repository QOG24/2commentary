Digital Culture — Case Study Dossier

Between the Click
and the Cause

The development of digital usage of nonprofit social welfare organisations: a look at Sciences Po Menton campus' societies

Designing Instagram posts. Administering a WhatsApp group. Coordinating a Zoom call. These aren't the first images that come to mind when you picture volunteer work — yet they are exactly what more and more people, students especially, end up spending their time doing once they join a charity. The gap between why people join (to help someone, directly, on the ground) and what they actually do (digital admin) can breed a quiet disconnection and disappointment that wears down a person's will to keep showing up. That paradox — a symptom of the wider digitalisation of work — is the question at the centre of this study.

Research question

How has the use of digital tools reshaped and impacted nonprofit social welfare organisations, focusing on the landscape of Sciences Po Menton campus' student societies?

This question opens onto the deeper reconfiguration these organisations have undergone since the digital turn at the start of the century.

Working hypotheses

Digitalisation is linked to the broader managerialisation and marketisation of work. Our hypothesis looks in two directions at once: the solidarity sector's goods and services have long sat outside market logic — until the tools it now relies on started pulling it back in.

What digital tools cost social work

Importing the logic they exist to resist

Nonprofits set out to counteract the harms of the market — yet the tools built by and for corporate efficiency carry a commodifying rationality straight into their daily practice.

A Customer Relationship Management system, built for sales teams, gets repurposed to "track engagement" — and beneficiaries and volunteers alike become a record to be optimised.

Efficiency at an ethical cost

Under pressure to prove their efficiency, social organisations adopt digital tools that come with costs the sector claims to stand against.

The water and energy footprint of AI systems, and beneficiary data handed to external, marketing-built platforms — inside a values-driven, supposedly non-exploitative sector.

Alienation from the cause itself

Social workers absorb these transformations directly: a Marxist sense of alienation can set in, eroding the very solidarity spirit that brought them to the work.

The more a shift is spent on digital tools rather than face-to-face outreach, the further a volunteer drifts from the person their labour is meant to serve.

What they still make possible

Reach and efficiency

Digital tools let organisations support more beneficiaries at once, lower the barrier to entry for new volunteers, and open easier access to information for everyone involved.

Accountability, made visible

The same technologies help produce real impact data and invite feedback from recipients — accountability that is harder to build without them.

The source text

Clicktivism, Slacktivism and Connective Action

Dr Max Halupka — Senior Lecturer, University of Canberra School of Government. Published 2022 in The Elgar Encyclopedia of Technology and Politics, a survey of how technological change is reshaping political life.

  • Field Political science, media studies, political sociology
  • Method A literature map of online political participation, sorting how it has been categorised in order to weigh its scope and its use
  • Angle Descriptive rather than argumentative — the only real claim is that a "click's" political legitimacy depends entirely on which definition of activism you start from

Three ways of reading a click

The paper sorts online political participation into three competing accounts of what a click is actually worth.

Clicktivism — read as legitimate

A noncommittal, nonspecialised, easily replicated act that still engages a real political object (Halupka, 2014) — an impulsive form of expression now well settled into how citizens participate. It is carried by the spread of sociotechnical environments such as social media (Karpf, 2020), and it links individual gestures to collective movements in ways that unsettle older forms of participation.

  • Digitally situated
  • Impulsive
  • Noncommittal
  • Draws on no specialised knowledge
  • Easily replicated
  • Engages an existing, broader political object

Its limit: a low-risk, low-cost act, closer to personal satisfaction than tangible impact — signing a petition or sharing a post stands in for joining a party or a protest, a kind of performative activism.

Slacktivism — read as illegitimate

The critique of clicktivism: it names the inefficacy and self-interest running through clicktivist behaviour. Its real charge is that clicktivism moves people away from collective, on-the-ground engagement by convincing them a click already counts — breeding a political complacency that keeps genuine political consciousness from forming. The label only holds inside the social fields (Bourdieu) that already treat clicktivism as legitimate, and it presumes an open, democratic system to begin with.

Connective action — read as legitimate

A theory that treats online activism as an organising principle in its own right: communication itself functions as organisation, building relationships and moving information without needing a formal structure. It makes political engagement more horizontal, decoupled from traditional authorities and institutions.

Its limit: only a "thin community," held together by a shared concern rather than a shared ideology — more fragile than older forms of organising. Someone can back a charity for exiled people without ever joining the party that fights the imperialism and forced displacement behind the need for one.

Agent-based definitions

Clicktivism & connective action

Legitimacy comes from what the individual agent produces or clicks. The risk: missing how a single struggle sits inside a larger ideology — fragmenting political consciousness and collective action.

Structure-based definition

Slacktivism

Legitimacy can only come from broader collective action and ideology, never from an individual click — so online activism, on this account, has none.

What this means for our case study

Halupka's framework fits our question closely. Joining a nonprofit is itself a political act: people show up because a specific issue moved them, and acting on it is already an expression of political will. So what happens when organisations built on face-to-face relationships come to depend on virtual networks instead — can a screen replace that contact, or does it just produce a thinner kind of organisation, with less infrastructure and less action on the ground?

Menton sharpens the question. Most student societies working to support vulnerable communities, SPRH among them, operate in a small, peaceful city with an aging population above the national average, where executives and mid-career professionals make up more than a quarter of residents — the very communities these societies exist to reach are barely present locally. So most volunteer hours go instead to digital work: running meetings, assigning tasks on a shared spreadsheet, reaching out to speakers, drafting awareness material at a laptop.

A good share of that work reads as slacktivism in Halupka's sense — sharing posts and writing papers rather than joining a protest or donating time and money to larger charities, whether from a fear of repercussions from the university or a simple disinterest in more concrete, non-digital action. Most students also come from privileged backgrounds — under 3% of the 2026 intake arrived through the Convention d'Éducation Prioritaire pathway — which may explain why a modest level of engagement can already feel like enough.

From here, the project tests how far clicktivism, slacktivism and connective action actually describe what's happening on this campus — and whether digital tools have cost student societies some of their political relevance and impact along the way.
